The art of prompting

Before

Nothing says who the site is for or how it should look, so the AI has to guess.

part three

What a site can't do

Two limits of a plain website, and how a backend such as Supabase fills the gap.

I

It forgets everyone

Close the tab and a plain site has no idea who you were. It cannot keep users or saved games.

II

It knows only its files

It cannot show anything live, like the weather. It only shows what was typed into the code.

How logging in works

Sign up

A new row goes into a user table, with your email and a scrambled password.

Log in

The site checks your details. If they match, your browser gets a token.

Log out

The token is thrown away, and the site stops recognising you.

A token is a temporary sticker that says "this is me", so you don't retype your password on every click. It expires on its own. Logging in is just an API call. Supabase provides logins, a user database and usage statistics, and connects to GitHub, Vercel and Lovable.

my reflection

What stayed with me

The biggest lesson was about prompting. "Make a website for my course" gets something generic. A prompt with a role, a goal, an audience, the pages I need and a design style gets something I can use. The quality of what the AI builds depends on how clearly I describe what I want.

I also understood how the pieces connect. GitHub stores the code, but nothing there is a website yet. Vercel turns the repository into a live link and rebuilds it every time I push. Supabase covers what a plain site cannot do: remember users and save data.

Building Fashion Annotations in Lovable showed me that an idea can become a working prototype inside one class. Lovable handled the GitHub connection, so I could spend my time on the tone and the visual system.

The AI writes the code. The brief is still mine to write.

    part one

    A team, not a chatbot

    Each role gets its own conversation and its own skill, and every conversation pulls from the same project context.

    ( The planning team )

    I

    Brief Writer

    Drafts the brief from the project context. Needs the goal, audience, budget and timing.

    Output · draft brief
    II

    Brief Validator

    Critiques the brief in a fresh chat. Checks the objective, audience, message and metric.

    Output · verdict and top fixes
    III

    Strategist

    Digests the validated brief and develops the Big Idea.

    Output · Big Idea one-pager

    ( The production team )

    IV

    Art Director

    Creates the key visuals, using connectors such as Higgsfield.

    Output · key visual concepts and assets
    V

    Copywriter

    Builds the messaging hierarchy and adapts it for each channel and platform.

    Output · channel-by-channel copy
    VI

    Reviewer

    Checks the work against brand voice and confirms it delivers the brief and the Big Idea.

    Output · edits and sign-off

    part three

    Setting it up in Claude

    A project holds the context, skills hold the roles, and a scheduled task starts the chain.

    The project

    One Claude project, such as "LVMH AI Marketing Team", holds the instructions, context files and memory. Every conversation pulls from it.

    The skills

    Each role is a skill that states its purpose, when to use it and the inputs it needs. A skill works in every chat in the project.

    The trigger

    A scheduled task runs the Marketing Brief Builder every day. It reads the latest email labelled "Marketing Brief" and drafts the brief.

    The case: Terra

    A live demo on a fictional teaching brief: launching Terra, an invented Louis Vuitton handbag, on social media in Singapore.

    BriefvalidateRevised briefresearchStrategymessageHierarchyvisualiseKey visualsscoreReview

    The layers"The Art of Renewal" is the visual territory. "Made to Be Kept" is the proof pillar.

    my reflection

    What stayed with me

    This was one of my favourite sessions so far, and I wish we could have listened to Nick for longer. The biggest shift for me was to stop thinking of AI as one chatbot and start thinking of it as a team. A Brief Writer, a Validator, a Strategist, an Art Director, a Copywriter and a Reviewer each get their own conversation and their own skill, and they all work from the same project context. It is how an agency works, rebuilt inside Claude.

    The detail I keep coming back to is the fresh chat. A validator that reads the brief with fresh eyes catches what the writer missed, and a person still approves the plan before anything gets made. CRAFT gave me a simple way to check my own prompts: context, role, action, format and a test.

    The Terra demo connected straight to Fashion Annotations. Luxury strategy, heritage and social performance are what I write about, and seeing a 4.3 post lose to a 6.7 close-up made the point clearly: consumers never see the brief, only the creative. Next, I want to set up my own project with research and annotation skills, with one project per category, such as perfume and couture.

    Different conversations, one shared context.
